## Exciting archaeological finds

There is something about the sheltered coves and the proximity to good
fishing grounds that has led people, as far back as the Stone Age, to
settle here.

Remains of settlements from the Viking Age have been found on Røvær,
and it is known that during the herring fisheries in the early 1800s,
as many as 20,000 fishermen could be stationed on Røvær. They have all
left their mark.

In 1988, interesting archaeological finds were made on Røvær: a Stone
Age settlement, burial cairns, stone arrangements, clearance cairns,
rock shelters and sacrificial finds. At Grønavikjo, the remains of a
prehistoric fishing station with no fewer than 14 house foundations
were found, the oldest from the late Iron Age. They appear to have been
in use from the Migration Period until the early Middle Ages, and show
that organised seasonal fishing existed along the coast even in
prehistoric times.

## A rich fishing community

In the mid-1800s, Norway's population grew sharply, and people were on
the move in search of a livelihood. This coincided with rich herring
fisheries in the district. On Røvær, the fishing grounds were close by
and the harbour excellent. That is why new people settled here.

## Tragic accident in 1899

![Waves breaking over the smooth coastal rocks on a grey day](sjosproyt)

The 13th of October 1899 changed life on Røvær in an instant, and made
the island's name known across the whole country. Of the island's
roughly 130 inhabitants, 30 perished on their way home from a funeral
at Udland cemetery in Skåre municipality (now Haugesund). Twelve widows
were left behind with 36 fatherless children, none yet confirmed.
Almost everyone on the island lost someone of their own. The whole
country was mobilised on hearing of the accident, and fundraising
campaigns were started to support the surviving widows and children.
Røvær rose again, and the accident created a solidarity and care for
one another that remains strong and alive to this day.

The population peaked around 1950, at about 170 permanent residents.
Then the herring fisheries declined. Boats were sold, several families
moved away, and since then the population has inevitably varied over
time.

## Hiltahuset

Hiltahuset is the oldest house on Røvær. Its construction suggests it was
built around 1820-1830, from two older living rooms with a hall and
kitchen between them. It was built as a farmhouse and later served as a
retirement house for the older generation. In the 1880s one of the rooms
was at times used as a schoolroom: the teacher spent two weeks on Røvær
and two weeks at Hauge.

There are no papers on the house. It passed from mother to daughter by
word of mouth for several generations, and is named after Johan Hilt from
Bergen, who married one of the daughters of the house. Until around 1960
it was a holiday home.

Today Hiltahuset is a museum, furnished with objects and fittings that
show how people lived and built on Røvær, and one of its rooms holds an
exhibition about the accident in 1899.
